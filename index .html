A plantation looks uniform from the ground. From orbit, it doesn't.

Walk through a thousand hectares and every tree looks like the last. But invasive species rarely arrive all at once — they arrive at the edges, along waterlines and access roads, one seed at a time, and by the time a walking survey catches the pattern, it has already taken root through the block. Canopy Atlas checks the whole plantation on every pass, so encroachment is caught at the tree level, not the hectare level.

Four things the canopy tells us

Branch architecture

Indigenous species branch true to their form — consistent forking angles, even internode spacing. Many invasive species bolt for canopy space instead: long single runs, sparse forking, an asymmetric reach toward open light.

native pattern invasive pattern

Leaf and foliar form

Shape, size, edge serration, waxy versus matte reflectance in the visible and near-infrared bands — foliage carries a spectral fingerprint that holds steady across a species, whatever the light does that afternoon.

native leaf invasive leaf

Thermal signature

Dense native canopy holds moisture and transpires steadily, running cooler through the midday heat. Thinner, faster-growing invasive canopy loses that buffer — a warmer patch inside an otherwise even thermal field.

cooler / dense warmer / thin

Stem count and density

Every canopy crown in the block is segmented and counted automatically, giving true stems-per-hectare and a running rate of change — so a slow invasive foothold shows up as a trend line, not a surprise.

counted, native counted, invasive

One scan, one map

All four readings feed a single classification model, trained block by block on the species already confirmed in your plantation. The output is a per-tree map — not a heatmap of probability, but a call: native, invasive, or worth a closer look — handed to your ground team with coordinates attached.

Satellite observation

Multiple Earth-observation layers can be combined to track vegetation structure, spectral response and thermal variation over time.

What data can be used?
Optical multispectral imagery can provide visible and near-infrared information, while thermal layers add temperature context. Higher-resolution commercial imagery may be used when individual crowns need finer separation.
🌿

Species intelligence

The model compares canopy shape, spectral behaviour and spatial context against a plantation-specific reference library.

How is confidence handled?
Each classification can carry a confidence score. Low-confidence crowns are routed into a “closer look” class rather than being forced into native or invasive categories.
🗺️

Action-ready mapping

Instead of returning only a broad heatmap, the platform turns detections into locations that field teams can investigate.

What can the team receive?
A block-level dashboard, flagged coordinates, change-over-time views, priority zones and exportable records for follow-up surveys.

Data sources & methodology

Canopy Atlas is designed as a data-fusion concept: satellite observations provide repeat coverage, while field-confirmed reference trees make the classification plantation-specific.

Satellite imagery
The interface is ready to connect to public Earth-observation catalogues and, where available, higher-resolution imagery providers. The current HTML is a front-end prototype and does not claim to fetch live satellite tiles by itself.
Change detection
Repeat observations can be compared to identify new canopy gaps, density shifts, boundary expansion and persistent spectral or thermal anomalies.
Ground truth & validation
Field observations are important for training and validating species-level models. The “no ground survey” wording in the original concept should therefore be treated as a product goal rather than a scientific guarantee.
Important prototype note
Values such as 94.2%, 0.4 ha and 72 hr are retained as prototype/interface values from the supplied concept. They are not presented here as independently validated performance claims.
